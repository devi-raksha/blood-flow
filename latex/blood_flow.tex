\begin{abstract}
This work presents a Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method for one-dimensional blood flow in arterial networks with Windkessel boundary conditions. The scheme uses an Harten--Lax--van Leer (HLL) numerical flux and implicit time integration. After spatial discretization, the complete network---including cell states, hybrid traces, junction conditions, and dynamic RCR terminal pressures---is written as a residual index-one differential--algebraic equation (DAE) and integrated with variable-step, variable-order backward differentiation formulas. The implicit formulation removes the explicit CFL stability restriction associated with explicit time integration, while accuracy and nonlinear convergence still control the admissible step size. The central contribution is a single global residual and Jacobian for the network and its terminal dynamics. This residual/Jacobian structure also provides a natural interface for future monolithic coupling with external nonlinear elasticity models. We establish local solvability and a semi-discrete energy estimate, and show optimal convergence rates for both solution variables in the $L^2$ and $H^1$ norms. The solver is validated against established benchmarks, including an aortic bifurcation and a $37$-artery network, and is used for strong-scaling studies on networks containing $8\,\mathrm{k}$ anatomical and up to $100\,\mathrm{k}$ synthetic vessels. The paper identifies the flow-side residual formulation for future external-elasticity coupling.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
One-dimensional ($1\mathrm{D}$) models of the cardiovascular system provide a computationally efficient framework for analyzing pulse-wave propagation and hemodynamic variables across complex arterial networks \cite{formaggia2003one,sherwin2003computational,blanco2025one,kim2025one,boileau2015benchmark}. By simplifying the three-dimensional Navier--Stokes equations through cross-sectional averaging, these reduced-order models retain the dominant network-scale dynamics at a small fraction of the cost of fully three-dimensional simulations. In the literature, each vessel is commonly represented as an interval with a single axial coordinate. Anatomically realistic arteries, however, are curved tubes with a definite orientation in space, and effects such as gravity depend on that orientation. In existing $1\mathrm{D}$ models, gravity is typically introduced through a source term involving the component of gravitational acceleration along the vessel axis \cite{MR5106706,shramko2023gravity}.

In view of possible coupling with three-dimensional elasticity models~\cite{MR1873203,heltai2021multiscale,belponer2023reduced}, we incorporate the orientation into the generic formulation while retaining a genuinely one-dimensional network discretization. Each vessel is parameterized along a centerline curve embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ through a directional field $\bm{b}(\bm{x})$, so the flux divergence takes the variable-coefficient form $\nabla \cdot (\bm{b}(\bm{x}) F(\mathbf{w}))$; the full model is given in Section~\eqref{sec:model-formulation}. This is a purely \emph{geometric} embedding. The present model describes one-dimensional flow along the embedded centerlines. The field $\bm b(\bm x)$ describes the local vessel orientation. When gravity is included, its projection onto the vessel enters the momentum source through $\bm g\!\cdot\!\bm b(\bm x)$. A spatially varying $\bm b(\bm x)$ therefore allows this known gravity-dependent source term to follow the vessel geometry accurately.

 A network model is closed by two types of coupling. At each junction one imposes conservation of mass and continuity of total pressure across the incident vessels. At each terminal we attach a lumped $0\mathrm{D}$ Windkessel model whose pressure satisfies an ordinary differential equation (ODE). Existing network formulations include characteristic, finite-volume, and discontinuous Galerkin discretizations, as well as hybrid $1\mathrm{D}$--$0\mathrm{D}$ models that couple different vessel descriptions through conservation conditions \cite{formaggia2003one,ghitti2023hybrid}. Previous studies already include terminal coupling. The contribution here is the simultaneous treatment of all vessel, junction, and terminal equations as one residual index-one differential--algebraic (DAE) system. The system is integrated implicitly by a variable-order backward differentiation formula ($\mathrm{BDF}$). Terminal pressures are included among the global unknowns on the same footing as the hybrid traces, so one global nonlinear system and one Jacobian resolve vessels, junctions, and terminals together. The time-step solve may require several Newton iterations; all of them act on this global system.

Realising that to implement this strategy, we need to have the appropriate global set of unknowns that couples naturally with hybrid traces junctions and terminals within the same nonlinear system, and the hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method provides exactly that. In practice, Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods are popular for hyperbolic conservation laws because they handle shocks and complex geometries well and parallelize nicely. However, in a standard DG formulation, the numerical flux on each element depends on its neighboring element states, leading to a globally coupled system involving all element degrees of freedom. Solving that requires inverting one enormous global system. HDG methods, fix this by introducing extra "trace" unknowns living only on the mesh skeleton (namely the trace of the unknown on the mesh faces). Once those trace values are known, each element can be solved completely independently through local element solver \cite{MR3345205}. Since the junction and terminal conditions involve the skeleton unknowns, they can be added straight into the trace system, and a single Newton step then solves the
traces, junctions, and terminals at once.
\noindent

HDG methods have been extensively studied over the last two decades, and several HDG formulations incorporating approximate
Riemann solvers such as the Harten--Lax--van Leer (HLL)
flux have been proposed for hyperbolic systems,
particularly in the context of compressible fluid
dynamics \cite{peraire2010hybridizable,vila2021hybridisable}.
For $1\mathrm{D}$ blood flow, DG methods have been used for reduced models \cite{puelz2017comparison,masri2022discontinuous} and for coupled flow and solute transport on networks \cite{masri2022discontinuous}. To the best of our knowledge, the combination studied here---an HDG--HLL network discretization, multi-way Riemann-coupled junctions, dynamic Windkessel states, and a residual DAE formulation---has not, to date, been considered within a single solver architecture. The analytical difficulty is concentrated at the junctions: single-valued traces are sufficient for ordinary interfaces, whereas multiple connected vessels require the simultaneous imposition of mass conservation, total-pressure continuity, and characteristic compatibility across all incident vessels. The formulation also represents gravity through the source term $\bm g\!\cdot\!\bm b(\bm x)$, which accounts for the local vessel orientation. The demonstrated contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item An HDG--HLL discretization of the $1\mathrm{D}$ network that incorporates junctions and RCR terminals into one condensed trace system.
    \item An implicit index-one DAE formulation in which cell and terminal-capacitor variables are differential and hybrid traces are algebraic, together with local solvability and a semi-discrete energy estimate.
    \item Numerically optimal spatial convergence on a manufactured solution in the $L^2$ and $H^1$ norms for both conserved variables.
    \item Validation against the aortic bifurcation and $37$-artery network, together with strong-scaling studies on networks of up to $8\,\mathrm{k}$ anatomical vessels and $100\,\mathrm{k}$ synthetic vessels.
\end{enumerate}
The external-elasticity coupling is a target for future work. At the residual level, an external model with unknowns $z$ can be appended schematically as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{flow}}(t,y,\dot y;z)=0,\qquad
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{ext}}(t,z,\dot z;y)=0,
\qquad \mathcal C(y,z)=0,
\label{eq:future_external_coupling}
\end{equation}
so that the flow, elasticity, and interface equations are assembled into the same Newton system. This is the precise sense in which the DAE formulation provides a coupling interface; the coupled elasticity computation itself is left for future work.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model-formulation} presents the governing $1\mathrm{D}$ system, tube law, and characteristic structure. Section~\ref{sec:hdg-formulation} develops the HDG--HLL discretization. Section~\ref{sec:transmission} discusses the unified treatment of transmission, boundary, and junction conditions. In Section~\ref{sec:analysis} we establish solvability and energy stability of the semi-discrete scheme. Section~\ref{sec:time_integration} discusses the implicit time-integration strategy and the associated nonlinear solvers. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} presents convergence results, verification against benchmark problems, and strong-scaling studies for very large vascular networks.

\section{Governing equations}\label{sec:model-formulation}
We consider the one-dimensional blood flow equations parameterized along the centerline of each vessel, with tangent vector $\bm b(\bm x)$. The compact vector form of the conservation system is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{equation_system}
\partial_t \mathbf{w}
+\nabla\cdot\left(\bm b(\bm{x})\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{w})\right)
=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{w}),
\qquad
\mathbf{w}= \begin{pmatrix}
    A \\[1mm]
U
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the nonlinear physical flux and source term are given by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{w}) =
\begin{pmatrix}
F_A(\mathbf{w})\\
F_U(\mathbf{w})
\end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
AU\\[2mm]
\dfrac{U^2}{2}+\dfrac{1}{\rho}P(A)
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{w}) =
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\[2mm]
-\dfrac{fU}{A}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here $A(s,t)$ is the cross-sectional area, $U(s,t)$ is the average axial velocity, and $\rho$ is the blood density. The displayed source contains viscous friction. When a gravitational body force $\bm g$ is included, its momentum contribution is the known term $\bm g\!\cdot\!\bm b(\bm x)$. The field $\bm b(\bm x)$ describes the vessel direction and supplies the geometric projection used by this source term. The viscous friction coefficient is
$f = 2(\zeta+2)\,\mu\,\pi$,
where $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity and $\zeta$ is the velocity-profile
parameter. We formulate the blood-flow equations along the vessel direction.
The vector $\bm b$ describes the vessel direction. Although the divergence
form can be reduced to a derivative along the vessel, we retain it because it
explicitly represents the vessel orientation. In our simulations, the vessels
are straight and aligned with $\bm e_1$, but the formulation also applies to
vessels with a general orientation.

The arterial wall mechanics are governed by the tube law~\cite{matthys2007pulse}
\begin{align}\label{tube_law}
P(A,s) - P_0 = P_d + \frac{\beta(s)}{A_d(s)}
\left[ (A^m - A_d(s)^m) \right], \qquad \beta = \frac{4}{3} \sqrt{\pi} E h_w(s), \quad m = \frac{1}{2}
\end{align}
where $E$ is the wall Young's modulus,
$h_w$ is the wall thickness, and
$A_d(s)$ is the local diastolic area. For a tapered vessel,
$A_d(s) = \pi r_d(s)^2$, where $r_d$ is interpolated linearly between the
inlet radius $r_{\mathrm{in}}$ and the outlet radius $r_{\mathrm{out}}$
~\cite{alastruey2012arterial,boileau2015benchmark}. Thus,

\begin{equation}
  r_d(s) = r_{\mathrm{in}}
  + \frac{s - s_{\min}}{s_{\max} - s_{\min}}
  \bigl(r_{\mathrm{out}} - r_{\mathrm{in}}\bigr),
  \qquad
  A_d(s) = \pi\,r_d(s)^2,
  \label{eq:taper}
\end{equation}
where $s_{\min}$ and $s_{\max}$ are the arc-length bounds of the
vessel.
For a uniform vessel, $r_{\mathrm{in}} = r_{\mathrm{out}} = r_d$
and $A_d$ reduces to the constant $\pi r_d^2$.

The resulting wave speed is
\[
c(A,s) = \sqrt{\frac{A}{\rho}\,\partial_A P}
= \sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2\rho A_d}}A^{1/4}.
\]
The flux Jacobian is
\[
\frac{\partial \mathbf{F}}{\partial\mathbf{w}}= \begin{pmatrix}
 U  & A \\
 c^2/A  & U
\end{pmatrix}    
\]
It has eigenvalues $\lambda_{1,2}=U\pm c$. Under the physiological
condition $|U|<c$, the system in Eq.~\eqref{equation_system} is strictly
hyperbolic and has the associated Riemann invariants
$W_{1,2}=U\pm4(c-c_0)$, where $c_0=c(A_d,s)$.

\section{Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin Framework}\label{sec:hdg-formulation}
Let $\Th$ be the collection of non-overlapping line elements $K$, and let $\Eh$ be the set of all boundary endpoints (nodes), split $\Eh$ into three mutually disjoint subsets:
\[
  \Eh = \Eho \cup \Ehb \cup \Ehj,
\]
representing standard interior nodes ($\Eho$), exterior boundary terminals ($\Ehb$), and branching junctions ($\Ehj$). Following the standard HDG construction \cite{MR3345205,peraire2010hybridizable}, we introduce a local element space $\mathbf{W}_h^k$ and the trace space $\mathbf{M}_h^k$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{W}_h^k &= \left\{ \mathbf{v} \in (L^2(\Th))^2 : \mathbf{v}|_K \in (\mathcal{P}^k(K))^2, \, \forall K \in \Th \right\}, \\
  \mathbf{M}_h^k &= \left\{ \bm{\mu} \in (L^2(\Eh))^2 : \bm{\mu}|_F \in (\mathcal{P}^k(F))^2, \, \forall F \in \Eh \right\}.
\end{align*}
We seek $(\mathbf w_h,\who)\in\mathbf W_h^k\times\mathbf M_h^k$ with
$\who=(\widehat A_h,\widehat U_h)^\top$. 

Here note that, since the mesh is one-dimensional, every face $F \in \Eh $ is a single point. On the point, the polynomial space $\mathcal{P}^k(F)^2 = \mathbb R^2$. The trace space $\mathbf{M}_h^k$ is therefore independent of the polynomial degree $k$. On each face the trace is a single pair of values $\who (F)=(\widehat A_h,\widehat U_h)(F) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. We discuss the trace values at interior faces, boundary faces, and junctions in detail in Section~\ref{sec:transmission}.

Testing \eqref{equation_system} on each element and replacing boundary flux by local flux gives the local problem  
\begin{equation}  \label{eq:hdg_local}
  \left(\partial_t \mathbf{w}_h, \mathbf{v}\right)_K - \left(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{w}_h), \bm b \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v}\right)_K + \left\langle \widehat{\mathbf{F}}_h \cdot \bm n, \mathbf{v} \right\rangle_{\partial K} = \left(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{w}_h), \mathbf{v}\right)_K, \quad \forall \mathbf{v} \in (\mathcal{P}^k(K))^2,
\end{equation}
where $\bm n$ is the outward unit normal. The interior state on each element
is recovered from its trace, while the traces are fixed globally by the
transmission, boundary, and junction conditions. See Section~\ref{sec:transmission}.

For the configuration in Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation_dofs}(b), we have $3$ vessels and each vessel with $N_{\mathrm{el}}=2$ elements and $k=1$. Each element carries $2(k+1)=4$ unknowns. That gives $3\,N_{\mathrm{el}}\cdot2(k+1)= 24$ cell unknowns (black dots). For trace pairs: $3$ at the interior nodes, $3$ at inlet and outlet and $3$ at junction.  These gives $18$ trace unknowns. The two RCR outlets add $2$ capacitor pressures. The system therefore has $24+18+2=44$ Dofs total and in a similar way we can calculate DoF for $2$-way junction Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation_dofs}(a).

\subsection{Numerical trace flux}\label{sec:num-flux}
The numerical trace flux is a two-state function of the interior (self) state
$\wh$ and the trace state. On any face, it receives the interior element state
$\mathbf w_h=(A_h,U_h)^\top$ (the \emph{self} state, evaluated on the face
from inside $K$) and the single-valued trace
$\widehat{\mathbf w}_h=(\widehat A_h,\widehat U_h)^\top$ (the
\emph{exterior} state living on the face), and
returns a single normal flux,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  \;=\;
  \mathcal F_{\mathrm{num}}\bigl(\,b\!\cdot\!\bm n,\;\mathbf w_h,\;
  \widehat{\mathbf w}_h\,\bigr).
  \label{eq:numflux_two_state}
\end{equation}
We use $\mathcal F_{\mathrm{num}}$ to denote a standard numerical flux,
such as Lax--Friedrichs, Rusanov, Roe, or HLL. In HDG form, these fluxes can
be written as
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  \;=\;
  \underbrace{\mathbf F(\widehat{\mathbf w}_h)\cdot\bm n}_{\text{consistent part}}
  \;+\;
  \underbrace{\bm\Sigma(\mathbf w_h,\widehat{\mathbf w}_h)\,
  \bigl(\mathbf w_h-\widehat{\mathbf w}_h\bigr)}_{\text{stabilisation / dissipation}},
  \label{eq:hdg_template}
\end{equation}
where the physical flux is anchored at the trace. The matrix
$\bm\Sigma$ penalizes the interior--trace mismatch, controls the amount of
numerical dissipation, and contributes to the stability of the hybridized
formulation.

The simplest closure is the scalar Local Lax--Friedrichs (LLF) penalty
$\bm\Sigma_{\mathrm{LF}}=\alpha_{\mathrm{LF}}\,\mathbf I$ with
$$\alpha_{\mathrm{LF}}=\max\bigl(|U_h\pm c_h|,\,|\widehat U_h\pm\widehat
c_h|\bigr),$$ which spreads identical dissipation over both characteristic
directions. 

Unlike scalar Lax--Friedrichs stabilisation, the HLL flux uses separate estimates of the left- and right-running wave speeds. This allows the numerical flux to distinguish between the two characteristic directions and provide wave-dependent dissipation.

\textit{From the HDG stabilisation to the HLL flux.}
In the HDG context the self/interior state is the
left state and the trace is the right state,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf w_L \equiv \mathbf w_h = (A_h,U_h)^\top,
  \qquad
  \mathbf w_R \equiv \widehat{\mathbf w}_h = (\widehat A_h,\widehat U_h)^\top .
\end{equation}
The orientation-projected physical fluxes are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf F_\star\cdot\bm n
  =
  (b\!\cdot\!\bm n)
  \begin{pmatrix}
  A_\star U_\star \\[2pt]
  \tfrac12 U_\star^2 + P(A_\star)/\rho
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \star\in\{L,R\},
  \label{eq:proj_flux}
\end{equation}
with the wave speed from the tube law $c(A)$.
The signal speeds are estimated from the averaged states:
\begin{equation}
  \bar U=\tfrac12(U_L+U_R),
  \quad
  \bar c=\tfrac12\bigl(c(A_L)+c(A_R)\bigr),
  \qquad
  s_L=\bar U-\bar c,
  \quad
  s_R=\bar U+\bar c .
  \label{eq:wave_speeds}
\end{equation}
The HLL numerical flux is then the three-branch expression \cite[Chapter~10]{toro2013riemann}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hll_flux}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  =
  \begin{cases}
  \mathbf F_R\cdot\bm n, & s_R\le 0,\\[6pt]
  \dfrac{s_R\,\mathbf F_L\cdot\bm n - s_L\,\mathbf F_R\cdot\bm n
         + s_R s_L\,(\mathbf w_R-\mathbf w_L)}{s_R-s_L}, 
  & s_L<0<s_R, \\[6pt]
  \mathbf F_L\cdot\bm n, & s_L\ge 0.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In the subcritical physiological regime $|U|<c$, the
dissipative jump $s_Rs_L(\mathbf w_R-\mathbf w_L)$ acts on the
\emph{raw} states.

\textit{Equivalence with the HDG normal flux.}
The central branch of~\eqref{eq:hll_flux} is the numerical flux itself. It can
be decomposed into a consistent part and a \emph{scalar} dissipation acting on
the interior--trace jump. Splitting the
numerator,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  =
  \underbrace{\frac{s_R\,\mathbf F(\mathbf w_h)\cdot\bm n
            -s_L\,\mathbf F(\widehat{\mathbf w}_h)\cdot\bm n}{s_R-s_L}}
            _{\text{consistent (wave-weighted) flux}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\bm\Sigma_{\mathrm{HLL}}\,
  \bigl(\mathbf w_h-\widehat{\mathbf w}_h\bigr)}_{\text{dissipation}}, 
  \label{eq:hll_consistent}
\end{equation}
where $$
  \bm\Sigma_{\mathrm{HLL}}=\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}\,\mathbf I,
  \quad
  \delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}= -\frac{\,s_L\,s_R}{\,s_R-s_L\,}\;\ge 0 .$$
Here the stabilisation $\bm\Sigma_{\mathrm{HLL}}$ is the \emph{scalar} matrix
$\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}\mathbf I$: in subcritical flow $s_L<0<s_R$, so
$\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}=-s_Ls_R/(s_R-s_L)>0$. 

\begin{remark}[HLL $\to$ Rusanov $\to$ LLF]
With a single symmetric speed $s_R=-s_L=\alpha$, the average
in~\eqref{eq:hll_consistent} becomes $\tfrac12\bigl(\mathbf F(\mathbf
w_h)+\mathbf F(\widehat{\mathbf w}_h)\bigr)\cdot\bm n$ and
$\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}=\alpha/2$, recovering the Rusanov / local
Lax--Friedrichs flux
$\tfrac12\bigl(\mathbf F(\mathbf w_h)+\mathbf F(\widehat{\mathbf
w}_h)\bigr)\cdot\bm n+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\bigl(\mathbf w_h-\widehat{\mathbf
w}_h\bigr)$. HLL is therefore the two-wave generalisation that weights the
forward ($U+c$) and backward ($U-c$) characteristics. A single parameter $\alpha$ provides the common symmetric bound in the Rusanov / local Lax--Friedrichs flux.
\end{remark}

\section{Transmission, boundary and junction conditions}\label{sec:transmission}
The traces are determined by requiring transmission of the numerical trace flux: find
$\widehat{\mathbf w}_h\in\mathbf M_h^k$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{K\in\Th}
  \bigl\langle\widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n,\bm\mu\bigr\rangle
    _{\partial K\setminus(\Ehb\cup\Ehj)}
  +\sum_{F\in\Ehb}\bigl\langle\widehat{\mathbf B}_h,\bm\mu\bigr\rangle_F
  +\sum_{J\in\Ehj}\bigl\langle\widehat{\mathbf J}_h,\bm\mu\bigr\rangle_J
  =0,
  \qquad\forall\bm\mu\in\mathbf M_h^k .
  \label{eq:global_hdg}
\end{equation}
The same HLL flux \eqref{eq:hll_flux} is used in the local problem \eqref{eq:hdg_local} and in the interior transmission term, while the operators
$\widehat{\mathbf B}_h$ and $\widehat{\mathbf J}_h$
encode the boundary and junction physics defined next.

A key feature of the formulation is that the same HLL numerical
flux~\eqref{eq:hll_flux} is employed both in the local cell problem and in
the global transmission condition. This provides a consistent coupling
between the element-interior solution and the globally defined trace
unknowns, while allowing boundary and junction physics to be incorporated
through the operators $\widehat{\mathbf B}_h$ and
$\widehat{\mathbf J}_h$.
\subsection{Interior faces}\label{sec:interior}
For a face shared by $K_L$ and $K_R$ of the same vessel, with
$b\!\cdot\!\bm n_R=-\,b\!\cdot\!\bm n_L$, the residual
is the sum of the two one-sided HLL fluxes, each taking that side's interior as the self state and the shared trace as the exterior state:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal F_{\mathrm{int}}
  =
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\bigl(b\!\cdot\!\bm n_L,\,\mathbf w_L,\,
  \widehat{\mathbf w}_h\bigr)
  +
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\bigl(b\!\cdot\!\bm n_R,\,\mathbf w_R,\,
  \widehat{\mathbf w}_h\bigr)
  =\mathbf 0 .
  \label{eq:interior_hll}
\end{equation}
These are two scalar equations (area and momentum rows) fixing the two trace
unknowns $(\widehat A_h,\widehat U_h)$. 
\subsection{Physical boundaries: \texorpdfstring{$\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_h$}{B-hat}}\label{sec:boundaries}
At a subcritical boundary face, the trace is determined by one physical condition and one outgoing characteristic compatibility condition. The operator $\widehat{\mathbf B}_h$
combines it with one outgoing Riemann-invariant compatibility condition,
using $W_{1,2}=U\pm4(c-c_0)$ and $c_0=c(A_d)$.

\textit{Inflow.} A prescribed flow rate $Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$ with matched backward invariant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc_inflow}
  \widehat{\mathbf B}_h^{\mathrm{in}}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  \widehat A_h\,\widehat U_h - Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t)\\[6pt]
  \bigl[\widehat U_h-4(\widehat c_h-c_0)\bigr]
  -\bigl[U_h-4(c_h-c_0)\bigr]
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\mathbf 0 .
\end{equation}

\textit{Windkessel/RCR outlet.} Pressure continuity with the Windkessel
terminal is combined with the outgoing forward invariant:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc_rcr}
  \widehat{\mathbf B}_h^{\mathrm{rcr}}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  P(\widehat A_h) - \bigl(R_1\,\widehat A_h\widehat U_h + P_c\bigr)\\[6pt]
  \bigl[\widehat U_h+4(\widehat c_h-c_0)\bigr]
  -\bigl[U_h+4(c_h-c_0)\bigr]
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\mathbf 0 ,
\end{equation}
where the capacitor pressure $P_c$ is a global unknown in the DAE state. The
single-resistance outlet is obtained with $R_1\equiv R$ and $C=0$. The
capacitor pressure is determined by the additional DAE equation
\[
C\dot P_c = A\,U - \frac{P_c-P_{out}}{R_2},
\]
which we write as the DAE residual
\begin{equation}
R_{pc} = C \dot P_c  -
\widehat A_h\widehat U_h
+
\frac{P_c-P_{\mathrm{out}}}{R_2}.
\label{eq:rcr_capacitor}
\end{equation}
Because $P_c$ couples to the trace through \eqref{eq:bc_rcr}-\eqref{eq:rcr_capacitor},
the terminal contributes to the same Jacobian as the vessel variables. The
corresponding blocks $D$, $C$, and $E$ are defined in
Section~\ref{sec:time_integration}.

\textit{Reflection outlet.} A reflection coefficient $R_t$ sets the incoming
invariant from the outgoing one:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf B}_h^{\mathrm{refl}}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  \bigl[\widehat U_h+4(\widehat c_h-c_0)\bigr]-W_{1}^{\mathrm{int}}\\[6pt]
  \bigl[\widehat U_h-4(\widehat c_h-c_0)\bigr]-\bigl(-R_t\,W_{1}^{\mathrm{int}}\bigr)
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\mathbf 0,
  \qquad
  W_1^{\mathrm{int}}=U_h+4(c_h-c_0).
  \label{eq:bc_refl}
\end{equation}
In all cases the local cell equation on the face still uses the HLL
flux. At a boundary, the trace value is prescribed by
$\widehat{\mathbf B}_h$; at an interior interface, conservation
determines the trace.

\subsection{Junctions: \texorpdfstring{$\widehat{\mathbf J}_h$}{J-hat}}\label{sec:junctions}
Consider a junction $J$ connecting $K\ge 2$ vessels. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item For $K=2$, we define a two-way junction. In the implementation, if the two neighbouring elements sharing a face have the same tube law, the interface is treated as an interior trace. Otherwise, if the neighbouring elements belong to different vessels, the interface is classified as a junction and the corresponding junction conditions are imposed, which are given below.
\end{itemize}
Let
\[
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,i}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\widehat{A}_i \\ \widehat{U}_i
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad i = 1,\ldots,K,
\]
denote the hybrid trace state of vessel $i$ at the junction, with
orientation sign
\[
s_i =
\begin{cases}
+1, & \text{if vessel } i \text{ ends at the junction},\\
-1, & \text{if vessel } i \text{ starts at the junction}.
\end{cases}
\]

Following the standard $1\mathrm D$ junction closure
\cite{formaggia2003one,muller2016consistent}, mass conservation and
continuity of total energy head $\mathcal H(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,i})=\tfrac12\widehat U_i^2+\Psi(\widehat A_i)$,
$\Psi=P/\rho$, give the $K$ constraints
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_J
 (\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,K})
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}
s_i\,\widehat{A}_i\,\widehat{U}_i
\\[10pt]
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,1})
- \mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,2})
\\[4pt]
\vdots
\\[4pt]
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,1})
- \mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,K})
\end{pmatrix}
= \mathbf{0},
\qquad
\mathcal{H}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{h,i})
=
\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{U}_i^2 + \Psi(\widehat{A}_i),
\label{eq:junction_transmission}
\end{equation}
where $\Psi(A) = P(A)/\rho$. Row 1 enforces global mass
conservation; rows 2 to $K$ enforce continuity of total energy
head between all vessels and the reference vessel $i=1$.

\begin{remark}[Single-valued vs.\ multi-valued traces]
On interior faces and at physical boundaries the trace is single-valued, as in
standard HDG. At vessel junctions, each incident face carries its own trace
because the cross-sectional area may be discontinuous between branches. The
branch traces are coupled through conservation of mass, continuity of total
pressure, and Riemann invariants.
\end{remark}

\section{Conditional solvability and energy balance}\label{sec:analysis}
\textit{Notation.}
Throughout the section $\mathbf w=(A,U)^\top$ with $A>0$, and we write $Q=AU$
for the flow rate and $\widehat Q:=\widehat A\,\widehat U$ for its trace
counterpart. We set $\Psi(A):=P(A)/\rho$, denote the wave speed by
$c(A)=\sqrt{(A/\rho)P'(A)}$, and abbreviate
$\int_{\Th}(\cdot):=\sum_{K\in\Th}\int_K(\cdot)$. The admissible set is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:admissible}
  \mathcal U=\bigl\{(\wh,\who):\ A_h>\tfrac12A_{\min},\ \widehat A_h>\tfrac12A_{\min},\
  |U_h|<c_h,\ |\widehat U_h|<\widehat c_h\ \text{on }\Th\bigr\},
\end{equation}
that is, the discrete states with strictly positive area and strictly
subcritical flow. Positive area keeps the tube law and wave speed differentiable, while subcriticality makes the entropy of Section~\ref{sec:entropy} convex.

We use two algebraically equivalent representations of the numerical normal flux. The first is the
\emph{trace-anchored} form familiar from the HDG literature,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_anchored}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  =\sigma\,\mathbf F(\who)+\bm\Sigma(\wh,\who)\,(\wh-\who),
  \qquad \sigma:=\bm b\cdot\bm n,
\end{equation}
in which the physical flux is evaluated at the trace alone. The second is the
\emph{wave-weighted} HLL form \eqref{eq:hll_consistent} actually implemented
here, whose consistent part mixes the interior and trace states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hll_wave}
  \widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n
  =\frac{s_R\,\mathbf F(\wh)\!\cdot\!\bm n-s_L\,\mathbf F(\who)\!\cdot\!\bm n}
        {s_R-s_L}
  +\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}\bigl(\wh-\who\bigr),
  \qquad
  \delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}=-\frac{s_Ls_R}{s_R-s_L}\ \ge 0 .
\end{equation}
The trace-anchored matrix $\bm\Sigma$ in \eqref{eq:trace_anchored} incorporates the physical-flux difference between the interior and trace states. The scalar $\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}$ in \eqref{eq:hll_wave} is the coefficient of the interior--trace jump in the wave-weighted representation.

\subsection{Conditional solvability}\label{sec:solvability}

For any $(\wh,\who)\in\mathbf W_h^k\times\mathbf M_h^k$ the semi-discrete HDG
scheme reads
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\left(\partial_t\wh,\mathbf v\right)_K
- \left(\mathbf F(\wh),\bm b\cdot\nabla\mathbf v\right)_K
+ \left\langle\widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n,\mathbf v\right\rangle_{\partial K}
&= \left(\mathbf S(\wh),\mathbf v\right)_K,
\quad \forall\,\mathbf v\in(\mathcal P^k(K))^2,
\label{eq:semidiscrete1}\\
\sum_{K\in\Th}
\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n,\bm\mu\right\rangle
  _{\partial K\setminus(\Ehb\cup\Ehj)}
&+\sum_{F\in\Ehb}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf B}_h,\bm\mu\right\rangle_F
\nonumber\\
&+\sum_{J\in\Ehj}\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf J}_h,\bm\mu\right\rangle_J=0,
\qquad \forall\,\bm\mu\in\mathbf M_h^k .
\label{eq:semidiscrete2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Expanding the discrete fields in the finite-element bases produces coefficient
vectors $W\in\mathbb R^{N_c}$ and $\widehat W\in\mathbb R^{N_f}$, where $N_c$
and $N_f$ count the cell and trace degrees of freedom. Collecting the terminal
pressures into the differential block through $z=(W,P_c)$, the pair
\eqref{eq:semidiscrete1}--\eqref{eq:semidiscrete2} becomes a
differential--algebraic system,
\begin{equation}\label{dae_form}
\begin{aligned}
    M_z\,\dot z &= R(t,z,\widehat W),\qquad z=(W,P_c),\\
    0 &= G(t,z,\widehat W),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
in which $M_z=\mathrm{blockdiag}\bigl(M,\mathrm{diag}(C_i)\bigr)$ is positive
definite whenever the capacitive terminals satisfy $C_i>0$. The cell rows of $R$
carry the volume, flux and source contributions, its terminal rows are
$\widehat Q_i-(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})/R_{2,i}$, and the map $G$ gathers the
transmission, boundary and junction constraints. Purely resistive terminals enter through algebraic boundary conditions.

\begin{lemma}[Residual regularity]\label{lem:reg}
For the tube law~\eqref{tube_law} with fixed positive wall and reference-area
coefficients, and for $C^1$ prescribed data, the maps $R$ and $G$ are of class
$C^1$ on the admissible set~\eqref{eq:admissible}, extended by arbitrary
capacitor pressures. In particular they are locally Lipschitz in the state.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The set $\mathcal U$ is open, since the inequalities defining it are strict and
the discrete fields depend continuously on the coefficient vectors
$(W,\widehat W)$. Fix a compact $\mathcal K\subset\mathcal U$. Because the basis
functions are fixed, the maps $W\mapsto\wh$ and $\widehat W\mapsto\who$ are
linear, so only the elementwise nonlinearities require attention.

Strict positivity of the area is what makes those nonlinearities harmless. Every
state in $\mathcal K$ satisfies $A_h,\widehat A_h\ge\tfrac12A_{\min}>0$, so the
fractional powers and square roots appearing in the tube law, in
$c=\sqrt{AP'/\rho}$ and in their derivatives are well defined and continuously
differentiable there. Subcriticality plays a complementary role at the faces: it
places the averaged signal speeds strictly in the central branch of
\eqref{eq:hll_wave}, $s_L<0<s_R$, so the denominator $s_R-s_L$ is bounded away
from zero and the central branch applies throughout $\mathcal K$. Hence
$\widehat{\mathbf F}_h$ is a $C^1$ function of $(\wh,\who)$, and the same
reasoning applies verbatim to the boundary operator $\widehat{\mathbf B}_h$ and
the junction operator $\widehat{\mathbf J}_h$.

Finally, $R$ and $G$ are finite sums of integrals of compositions of these $C^1$
maps against fixed basis functions. Integration and finite summation preserve
differentiability, so $R,G\in C^1(\mathcal K)$; a $C^1$ map on a compact set has
bounded derivative, and the local Lipschitz estimate follows.
\end{proof}

The local solvability result assumes an invertible trace Jacobian. Remark~\ref{rem:scope} describes its boundary, junction and interior blocks.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional local existence and uniqueness]
\label{thm:existence_uniqueness}
Assume that
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(i)] the pressure law $P:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is of class $C^1$ with
    $P'(A)>0$, and $C_i,R_{2,i}>0$ at the capacitive terminals;
  \item[(ii)] the initial approximation is admissible, $A_h(\cdot,0)\ge A_{\min}>0$
    and $|U_h(\cdot,0)|<c_h(\cdot,0)$, and admits a consistent trace
    $\widehat W_0$ with $G(0,z_0,\widehat W_0)=0$;
  \item[(iii)] the trace Jacobian
    $D_0:=\partial_{\widehat W}G(0,z_0,\widehat W_0)$ is nonsingular,
    equivalently the semi-discrete system is index one at the initial state.
\end{itemize}
Then there exists $T^*>0$ such that the DAE~\eqref{dae_form} possesses a unique
solution $(z,\widehat W)\in C^1([0,T^*])$ which remains admissible; equivalently,
\eqref{eq:semidiscrete1}--\eqref{eq:semidiscrete2} admit a unique local solution
$(\wh,\who)$ in $\mathcal U$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1 (finite-dimensional DAE).}
Writing $\wh=\sum_{i=1}^{N_c}W_i(t)\phi_i$ and
$\who=\sum_{j=1}^{N_f}\widehat W_j(t)\psi_j$ and substituting into
\eqref{eq:semidiscrete1} produces the matrix form \eqref{dae_form}. The cell mass
matrix $M=(\phi_i,\phi_j)_{\Th}$ is symmetric positive definite because the local
basis functions are linearly independent, and $\mathrm{diag}(C_i)$ is positive
definite for $C_i>0$; hence $M_z$ is invertible.

\emph{Step 2 (elimination of the trace).}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:reg} the constraint map $G$ is $C^1$ in a neighbourhood of
$(0,z_0,\widehat W_0)$, and by hypothesis~(iii) its partial Jacobian $D_0$ is
invertible there. The implicit function theorem therefore supplies a
neighbourhood of $(0,z_0)$ and a unique $\Phi\in C^1$ with
$\Phi(0,z_0)=\widehat W_0$ and
\[
  G\bigl(t,z,\Phi(t,z)\bigr)=0 .
\]

\emph{Step 3 (reduced ODE and Picard--Lindel\"of).}
On that neighbourhood the algebraic constraint has been removed and
\eqref{dae_form} collapses to the ordinary differential equation
\[
  \dot z=M_z^{-1}R\bigl(t,z,\Phi(t,z)\bigr)=:\mathcal G(t,z),
  \qquad z(0)=z_0 .
\]
As a composition of $C^1$ maps with the constant matrix $M_z^{-1}$, the field
$\mathcal G$ is $C^1$ and hence locally Lipschitz in $z$. Picard--Lindel\"of then
gives $T^*>0$ and a unique $z\in C^1([0,T^*])$; setting
$\widehat W(t):=\Phi(t,z(t))$ yields $\widehat W\in C^1$ with
$G(t,z,\widehat W)=0$ throughout, so the pair solves \eqref{dae_form}
\cite[Chapter~VI.1]{MR2657217}.

\emph{Step 4 (admissibility).}
The set $\mathcal U$ is open and the solution is continuous in time, so after
shortening the interval if necessary the discrete state remains admissible.
Reversing the expansions of Step~1 returns the solution $(\wh,\who)$ with the
stated regularity.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Trace Jacobian blocks]\label{rem:scope}
The boundary and junction blocks admit explicit nonsingularity checks, while the interior blocks depend on the derivatives of the complete numerical flux.

\emph{Inflow.} Differentiating \eqref{eq:bc_inflow} and using
$d\widehat c/d\widehat A=\widehat c/(4\widehat A)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Din}
  D_{\mathrm{in}}
  =\begin{bmatrix}
     \widehat U_h & \widehat A_h\\[4pt]
     -\,\widehat c_h/\widehat A_h & 1
   \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \det D_{\mathrm{in}}=\widehat U_h+\widehat c_h>0 ,
\end{equation}
the sign of the off-diagonal entry following from the minus in front of the
$4(\widehat c_h-c_0)$ term of the outgoing invariant.

\emph{RCR outlet.} The same computation applied to \eqref{eq:bc_rcr} yields
$\det D_{\mathrm{rcr}}=P'(\widehat A_h)-R_1(\widehat U_h-\widehat c_h)>0$ for
every $R_1\ge0$, by subcriticality together with $P'>0$.

\emph{Junction.} Let $(\delta\widehat A_i,\delta\widehat U_i)$ be a perturbation
of the branch traces annihilating the linearised junction operator. The
homogeneous characteristic rows give
$\delta\widehat U_i+4s_i\,\delta\widehat c_i=0$ with
$\delta\widehat c_i=(c_i/4A_i)\,\delta\widehat A_i$, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:junclin}
  \delta\widehat U_i=-s_i\frac{c_i}{A_i}\,\delta\widehat A_i,
  \qquad
  \delta\mathcal H_i=\frac{c_i}{A_i}\bigl(c_i-s_iU_i\bigr)\delta\widehat A_i,
  \qquad
  s_i\,\delta\widehat Q_i=-\frac{A_i}{c_i}\,\delta\mathcal H_i .
\end{equation}
Continuity of the total head forces a single common value
$\delta\mathcal H_i\equiv\delta\mathcal H$, whereupon linearised mass
conservation $\sum_i s_i\delta\widehat Q_i=0$ reduces to
$-\bigl(\sum_i A_i/c_i\bigr)\delta\mathcal H=0$ and therefore
$\delta\mathcal H=0$. Since $|U_i|<c_i$ implies $c_i-s_iU_i>0$, the middle
identity in \eqref{eq:junclin} gives $\delta\widehat A_i=0$ and then
$\delta\widehat U_i=0$. The junction block is thus nonsingular, and the argument
uses subcriticality in an essential way.

\emph{Interior.} Differentiating the
wave-weighted form \eqref{eq:hll_wave} with respect to the trace produces, besides
the jump contribution $-\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}\mathbf I$, the physical-flux
derivative $-\sigma s_L\,\mathbf F'(\who)/(s_R-s_L)$ together with the
derivatives of $s_L$ and $s_R$ through the averaged state
$\bar U=\tfrac12(U_h+\widehat U_h)$, $\bar c=\tfrac12(c_h+\widehat c_h)$.
Moreover the two sides of an interior face are built from \emph{different}
averages and therefore carry different values of $\delta_{\mathrm{HLL}}$. These contributions enter the assembled interior block. Hypothesis~(iii) imposes nonsingularity of the full trace Jacobian.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Entropy structure}\label{sec:entropy}

The nonlinear system \eqref{equation_system} carries a natural entropy structure,
which supplies both an admissibility criterion for weak solutions and the
analytical framework used below
\cite{tadmor1987numerical,harten1998convex}. We introduce a convex entropy
$\eta$, its entropy flux $q$ and the entropy variables $\vartheta=\eta'$ through
the usual compatibility relation with the physical flux. For the blood-flow model
the entropy has a transparent mechanical meaning: it is the sum of the kinetic
energy of the fluid column and the elastic energy stored in the vessel wall
\cite{formaggia2003one,MR4570554}. This structure yields positivity of the
discrete energy and, in Section~\ref{sec:energy_balance}, an exact balance for
the semi-discrete scheme.

\begin{definition}[Entropy pair]\label{def:entropy_defs}
A convex function $\eta:\mathcal U\to\mathbb R$ and a flux
$q:\mathcal U\to\mathbb R$ form an entropy pair for
$\partial_t\mathbf w+\partial_s\mathbf F(\mathbf w)=\mathbf S(\mathbf w)$ if
\[
  \eta'(\mathbf w)^\top\mathbf F'(\mathbf w)=q'(\mathbf w)^\top .
\]
The entropy variables are $\bm\vartheta(\mathbf w):=\eta'(\mathbf w)$. Smooth
solutions then satisfy the additional conservation law
$\partial_t\eta+\partial_sq=\bm\vartheta^\top\mathbf S$ whenever the tube-law
coefficients are spatially constant. When they vary along the vessel, $\eta$ and
$q$ depend explicitly on $s$ and the identity acquires two extra terms,
\[
  \partial_t\eta+\partial_sq
  =\bm\vartheta^\top\mathbf S
   +(\partial_sq)_{\mathbf w}-\bm\vartheta^\top(\partial_s\mathbf F)_{\mathbf w};
\]
for the tube law \eqref{tube_law} with constant $P^\circ$ these two terms
cancel, both being equal to $Q\,(\partial_sP)_A/\rho$.
\end{definition}

Let $P^\circ:=P_{\mathrm{ext}}+P_d$, so that $P(A_d,s)=P^\circ$ in every vessel
by \eqref{tube_law}. Define the total head, the pressure potential and the
entropy density by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:entropy}
  \mathcal H(\mathbf w):=\tfrac12U^2+\Psi(A),\qquad
  \Pi(A):=\frac1\rho\int_{A_d}^{A}\bigl(P(a)-P^\circ\bigr)\,da,\qquad
  \eta(\mathbf w):=\tfrac12AU^2+\Pi(A),
\end{equation}
together with the entropy flux $q(\mathbf w):=Q\bigl(\mathcal H-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)$.
Two normalisations are built into \eqref{eq:entropy} and both are needed later:
the lower limit $A_d$ in the integral makes $\Pi(A_d)=0$, while measuring the
pressure from $P^\circ$ places the minimum of $\Pi$ at the diastolic area. The dynamics depend on $\Pi'$.

\begin{lemma}[Entropy structure of the blood-flow system]\label{lem:entropy_pair}
The pair $(\eta,q)$ of \eqref{eq:entropy} is an entropy pair in the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:entropy_defs}, with entropy variables
$\bm\vartheta(\mathbf w)=\eta'(\mathbf w)=\bigl(\mathcal H-P^\circ/\rho,\ Q\bigr)^\top$.
Moreover $\eta$ is strictly convex on $\mathcal U$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hessian}
  \nabla^2\eta(\mathbf w)=\begin{pmatrix}c^2/A&U\\ U&A\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \det\nabla^2\eta=c^2-U^2>0,
  \qquad \operatorname{tr}\nabla^2\eta=c^2/A+A>0,
\end{equation}
so $\nabla^2\eta$ is positive definite precisely for $|U|<c$. Finally,
\[
  \mathbf S(\mathbf w)^\top\bm\vartheta(\mathbf w)=-fU^2\le0.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\Pi'(A)=(P(A)-P^\circ)/\rho$, differentiation gives
$\eta_A=\tfrac12U^2+\Pi'(A)=\mathcal H-P^\circ/\rho$ and $\eta_U=AU=Q$, which is
$\bm\vartheta$ by definition. It is worth observing that the two components of
$\bm\vartheta$ are precisely the total head and the flow rate, that is, the two
quantities constrained by the junction conditions
\eqref{eq:junction_transmission}; Lemma~\ref{lemma:junction_energy} below is a
direct consequence of this coincidence.

For compatibility, recall
$\mathbf F'=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}U&A\\ P'/\rho&U\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. A
direct computation gives $\partial_Aq=\vartheta_1U+QP'/\rho$ and
$\partial_Uq=\vartheta_1A+QU$, that is $q'=\bm\vartheta^\top\mathbf F'$, so
$(\eta,q)$ is an entropy pair.

For convexity, $\eta_{AA}=\Pi''(A)=P'(A)/\rho=c^2/A$, $\eta_{AU}=U$ and
$\eta_{UU}=A$, which is \eqref{eq:hessian}. A symmetric $2\times2$ matrix with
positive trace and positive determinant has two positive eigenvalues, and both
invariants are positive exactly when $A>0$ and $|U|<c$. Equivalently, completing
the square,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posdef}
  \mathbf z^\top\nabla^2\eta\,\mathbf z
  =A\Bigl(z_U+\tfrac{U}{A}z_A\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{c^2-U^2}{A}\,z_A^2 ,
\end{equation}
which exhibits the quadratic form as a sum of two nonnegative contributions and
absorbs the cross term $2Uz_Az_U$ into the square for arbitrary $z_A,z_U$. Taking
$z_U=-\tfrac{U}{A}z_A$ reduces the form to $(c^2-U^2)z_A^2/A$, establishing the stated characterization. Finally
$\mathbf S^\top\bm\vartheta=(0,-fU/A)\cdot(\mathcal H-P^\circ/\rho,\,AU)^\top=-fU^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Convexity and the admissible set]\label{rem:convex_subcritical}
In the primitive variables $(A,U)$, positive definiteness of the entropy Hessian is equivalent to subcriticality. In the conservative variables $(A,Q)$ the corresponding entropy is strictly convex for every $A>0$. The admissible states of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_uniqueness} lie in the primitive-variable convexity region.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Positivity of the energy]\label{energy_positivity}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
  \mathscr E_h(t)=\int_{\Th}\eta(\wh)\,dx
  +\sum_{i\in\mathrm{RCR}}\frac{C_i}{2\rho}\bigl(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i}\bigr)^2 .
\end{equation}
Then $\Pi(A_d)=\Pi'(A_d)=0$ and $\Pi''(A)>0$ for all $A>0$. Consequently
$\eta(\mathbf w)\ge0$ and $\mathscr E_h(t)\ge0$ at every admissible discrete
state, with equality precisely at the rest state $A=A_d$, $U=0$,
$P_{c,i}=P_{\mathrm{out},i}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The potential $\Pi$ is a definite integral whose lower limit is $A_d$, so
$\Pi(A_d)=0$ by construction. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$\Pi'(A)=(P(A)-P^\circ)/\rho$, and $P(A_d)=P^\circ$ then yields $\Pi'(A_d)=0$.
Differentiating once more,
\[
  \Pi''(A)=P'(A)/\rho>0,\qquad A>0 .
\]
Hence $\Pi$ is strictly convex with a stationary point at $A_d$, which is
therefore its unique global minimum, so $\Pi(A)\ge\Pi(A_d)=0$ with equality only
at $A=A_d$. Since $\tfrac12AU^2\ge0$ for $A>0$, vanishing only when $U=0$, we
obtain $\eta\ge0$ with the stated equality case. The Windkessel contributions in
\eqref{eq:energy} are squares with $C_i\ge0$, and $\mathscr E_h\ge0$ follows by
integration and summation.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact discrete energy balance}\label{sec:energy_balance}

We now derive an exact discrete energy identity, assuming exact spatial integration and a constant reference pressure $P^\circ$. Write $f_{K,F}:=\widehat{\mathbf F}_h\cdot\bm n$ for the
complete numerical normal flux on the face $F$ of the element $K$, and
$\sigma_{K,F}:=\bm b\cdot\bm n$ for the associated orientation.

\begin{lemma}[Discrete energy identity]\label{lemma:eta_inequality}
Let $(\wh,\who)\in\mathcal U$ solve \eqref{eq:semidiscrete1}--\eqref{eq:semidiscrete2}
with polynomial degree $p=k$, and let $v_h$ denote the elementwise $L^2$
projection of $\bm\vartheta(\wh,s)$ onto $\mathbf W_h^k$. Define the flux defect
and the volume defect
\begin{align}
  R_h&:=\sum_{K}\sum_{F\subset\partial K}
    \Bigl[\,v_h(F)^\top f_{K,F}
      -\sigma_{K,F}\,q(\widehat{\mathbf w}_{K,F},F)\Bigr],
  \label{eq:Rh}\\[2pt]
  V_h&:=\sum_{K}\Bigl[\bigl(\mathbf F(\wh,s),\partial_sv_h\bigr)_K
      +\bigl(\mathbf S,\,v_h-\bm\vartheta(\wh,s)\bigr)_K\Bigr].
  \label{eq:Vh}
\end{align}
Then, with $f$ the friction coefficient of Section~\ref{sec:model-formulation},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semi_discrete_energy}
  \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Th}\eta(\wh,s)
  +\int_{\Th}fU_h^2
  +\sum_{K}\sum_{F\subset\partial K}\sigma_{K,F}\,q(\widehat{\mathbf w}_{K,F},F)
  +R_h=V_h .
\end{equation}
For $p=0$ with coefficients constant on each element one has $v_h=\bm\vartheta(\wh)$
and $V_h=0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Test \eqref{eq:semidiscrete1} with $\mathbf v=v_h$. Since
$\partial_t\wh\in\mathbf W_h^k$, projection orthogonality removes the projection
error from the time term,
\[
  \bigl(\partial_t\wh,v_h\bigr)_K=\bigl(\partial_t\wh,\bm\vartheta\bigr)_K
  =\int_K\partial_t\eta=\frac{d}{dt}\int_K\eta ,
\]
while $\bm\vartheta^\top\mathbf S=-fU_h^2$ from Lemma~\ref{lem:entropy_pair} gives
$(\mathbf S,v_h)_K=-\int_KfU_h^2+(\mathbf S,v_h-\bm\vartheta)_K$. Summing the cell
equations over all $K$ and collecting the volume and projection remainders into
$V_h$,
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Th}\eta+\int_{\Th}fU_h^2
  +\sum_{K,F}v_h(F)^\top f_{K,F}=V_h .
\]
Adding and subtracting the physical trace entropy fluxes
$\sigma_{K,F}\,q(\widehat{\mathbf w}_{K,F})$ inside the face sum, and invoking the
definition \eqref{eq:Rh}, yields \eqref{eq:semi_discrete_energy}.

For $p=0$ with elementwise constant coefficients the function
$\bm\vartheta(\wh,s)$ already lies in $\mathbf W_h^0$, so $v_h=\bm\vartheta(\wh)$,
$\partial_sv_h=0$ and $v_h-\bm\vartheta=0$, whence $V_h=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Entropy-variable jumps]\label{rem:nojump}
The exact vector identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvt}
  \bm\vartheta(\mathbf w)-\bm\vartheta(\mathbf z)
  =\int_0^1\nabla^2\eta\bigl(\mathbf z+\theta(\mathbf w-\mathbf z)\bigr)
    (\mathbf w-\mathbf z)\,d\theta
\end{equation}
gives a nonnegative pairing with $\mathbf w-\mathbf z$ whenever the joining segment lies in the convexity region. An entropy-conservative two-state flux satisfies the entropy-potential jump condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ecjump}
  \bigl(\bm\vartheta(\mathbf w)-\bm\vartheta(\mathbf z)\bigr)^\top
  f^{\,\mathrm{ec}}_n(\mathbf w,\mathbf z)
  =\sigma\bigl(\psi(\mathbf w)-\psi(\mathbf z)\bigr),
  \qquad \psi:=\bm\vartheta^\top\mathbf F-q,
\end{equation}
with $\psi$ the entropy potential \cite{tadmor1987numerical}. For the HLL scheme, $R_h$ collects the complete wave-weighted numerical flux, including its physical-flux and jump contributions.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Physical trace energy at junctions]\label{lemma:junction_energy}
Assume the junction conditions \eqref{eq:junction_transmission} impose global
mass conservation $\sum_{i\in J}s_i\widehat Q_i=0$ together with continuity of
the total head. Then the consistent junction energy flux vanishes,
\[
  \Sigma_J:=\sum_{i\in J}s_i\,q(\widehat{\mathbf w}_i)=0 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Continuity of the head means that all branches share one value,
$\mathcal H(\widehat{\mathbf w}_i)\equiv\mathcal H^*$. That common value can then be
factored out of the sum, and mass conservation finishes the argument:
\[
  \Sigma_J=\bigl(\mathcal H^*-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)\sum_{i\in J}s_i\widehat Q_i
  =\bigl(\mathcal H^*-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)\sum_{i\in J}s_i\widehat A_i\widehat U_i=0 .
  \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Total-head coupling]\label{rem:head}
Common total head factors the physical trace energy flux as $(\mathcal H^*-P^\circ/\rho)\sum_i s_i\widehat Q_i$. The numerical-flux contribution is collected in $R_h$. The benchmarks in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_results} use the total-head condition \eqref{eq:junction_transmission}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Energy balance with numerical-flux defects]\label{thm:energy_balance}
On the local admissible interval of Theorem~\ref{thm:existence_uniqueness},
assume junctions as in Lemma~\ref{lemma:junction_energy}, prescribed-flow inlets
and capacitive RCR outlets with constant $P_{\mathrm{out},i}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy_balance}
  \frac{d}{dt}\mathscr E_h
  +\int_{\Th}fU_h^2
  +\sum_{i\in\mathrm{RCR}}
    \left[\frac{R_{1,i}\widehat Q_i^{\,2}}{\rho}
      +\frac{(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2}{\rho R_{2,i}}\right]
  =\mathcal B_\partial+V_h-R_h ,
\end{equation}
with $\mathscr E_h$ defined in \eqref{eq:energy} and $R_{1,i}\ge0$. The friction
and resistive contributions on the left are nonnegative. The boundary term
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary_term}
  \mathcal B_\partial
  =\sum_{\mathrm{inlets}}Q_{\mathrm{in}}
     \bigl(\widehat{\mathcal H}-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)
   -\sum_{i\in\mathrm{RCR}}\widehat Q_i
     \left[\tfrac12\widehat U_i^2
       +\frac{P_{\mathrm{out},i}-P^\circ}{\rho}\right]
\end{equation}
and $V_h-R_h$ collect the boundary power and numerical-flux and volume contributions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Start from \eqref{eq:semi_discrete_energy} and evaluate the physical trace
entropy flux sum $\sum_{K,F}\sigma_{K,F}q(\widehat{\mathbf w}_{K,F})$ face by
face. On an ordinary interface the trace is single-valued and
$\sigma_L=-\sigma_R$, so the two contributions cancel in pairs. At a junction
they cancel by Lemma~\ref{lemma:junction_energy}. At an inlet, where
$\sigma=-1$, the contribution is
$-Q_{\mathrm{in}}\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal H}-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)$.

The outlets require a short computation. Expanding the flux at an RCR face,
where $\sigma=+1$, and inserting the pressure-continuity row
$P(\widehat A_i)=R_{1,i}\widehat Q_i+P_{c,i}$ of \eqref{eq:bc_rcr},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:out1}
  \widehat Q_i\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal H}_i-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)
  =\widehat Q_i\left(\tfrac12\widehat U_i^2
     +\frac{P(\widehat A_i)-P^\circ}{\rho}\right)
  =\tfrac12\widehat Q_i\widehat U_i^2
   +\frac{R_{1,i}\widehat Q_i^{\,2}}{\rho}
   +\frac{\widehat Q_i\bigl(P_{c,i}-P^\circ\bigr)}{\rho}.
\end{equation}
Only the last term still involves the terminal unknown, and it is eliminated by
the Windkessel equation itself. Multiplying
$C_i\dot P_{c,i}=\widehat Q_i-(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})/R_{2,i}$ by
$(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})/\rho$, and using
$(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})\dot P_{c,i}
=\tfrac12\tfrac{d}{dt}(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2$ for constant
$P_{\mathrm{out},i}$, gives the energy law of the terminal,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:out2}
  \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{C_i}{2\rho}(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2\right]
  +\frac{(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2}{\rho R_{2,i}}
  =\frac{\widehat Q_i}{\rho}\bigl(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i}\bigr),
\end{equation}
that is, storage rate plus resistive loss equals the power received from the
vessel. Solving \eqref{eq:out2} for $\widehat Q_iP_{c,i}/\rho$ and substituting
into \eqref{eq:out1},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:out3}
  \widehat Q_i\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal H}_i-P^\circ/\rho\bigr)
  =\frac{d}{dt}\!\left[\frac{C_i(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2}{2\rho}\right]
   +\underbrace{\frac{R_{1,i}\widehat Q_i^{\,2}}{\rho}
     +\frac{(P_{c,i}-P_{\mathrm{out},i})^2}{\rho R_{2,i}}}_{\ \ge0}
   +\widehat Q_i\left[\tfrac12\widehat U_i^2
     +\frac{P_{\mathrm{out},i}-P^\circ}{\rho}\right].
\end{equation}
Each of the three groups in \eqref{eq:out3} has a destination. The first is
exactly the time derivative of the terminal part of $\mathscr E_h$ in
\eqref{eq:energy} and is absorbed into $\tfrac{d}{dt}\mathscr E_h$; the second is
nonnegative and stays on the left of \eqref{eq:energy_balance}; the third,
together with the inlet contribution, is transposed to the right and constitutes
$\mathcal B_\partial$ as given in \eqref{eq:boundary_term}. Substituting back into
\eqref{eq:semi_discrete_energy} proves the balance.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Terminal boundary power]\label{rem:kinetic_efflux}
The final bracket in \eqref{eq:out3} contains the kinetic efflux $\tfrac12\widehat Q_i\widehat U_i^2$ and the pressure-reference work $\widehat Q_i(P_{\mathrm{out},i}-P^\circ)/\rho$. These contributions enter $\mathcal B_\partial$ explicitly.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Closed network, conditional decay]\label{closed_network}
If $\Ehb=\emptyset$, then $\mathcal B_\partial\equiv0$ and
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}\mathscr E_h+\int_{\Th}fU_h^2=V_h-R_h .
\]
The discrete energy is therefore non-increasing provided $R_h-V_h\ge0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Solution-dependent bound]\label{given_inflow}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:energy_balance},
\begin{equation}\label{standard_energy}
  \mathscr E_h(t)\le\mathscr E_h(0)
  +\int_0^t\Bigl(|\mathcal B_\partial|+|V_h|+|R_h|\Bigr)\,d\tau,
  \qquad 0\le t\le T^* .
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Discard the nonnegative friction and resistive terms in
\eqref{eq:energy_balance} and integrate in time.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Boundary-state dependence]\label{rem:notclosed}
The right-hand side of \eqref{standard_energy} depends on the solution through the boundary states and numerical defects. At an inlet, \eqref{eq:bc_inflow} couples the prescribed flow to the outgoing interior invariant through
\[
  \widehat A_F\Bigl[W_2^{\mathrm{int}}+4\bigl(c(\widehat A_F)-c_0\bigr)\Bigr]
  =Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t).
\]
This relation determines the inlet state and hence its total head. The bound applies on the local admissible interval $[0,T^*]$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Gravitational source]\label{rem:gravity}
When a gravitational body force is included, the momentum source carries the
extra term $\bm g\cdot\bm b(\bm x)$, contributing the power
$\int_{\Th}(\bm g\cdot\bm b)\,Q_h$ to the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:semi_discrete_energy} and \eqref{eq:energy_balance}, where it is
collected in $V_h$. Its absolute value contributes to the right-hand side of \eqref{standard_energy}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Polynomial degree and energy diagnostics]\label{rem:scopek}
For $p\ge1$ and for spatially varying tube-law coefficients, the projection in Lemma~\ref{lemma:eta_inequality} retains the volume term $V_h$. The computed energy $\mathscr E_h(t)$ and the residual of \eqref{eq:energy_balance} provide diagnostics for the time-discrete solution.
\end{remark}
\section{Time Integration}\label{sec:time_integration}

The semi-discrete problem has the residual DAE form
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf F(t,\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y})=\mathbf 0
  \label{eq:time_dae}
\end{equation}
with the differential and algebraic components identified below. Let $t_n$ be
the current time, $h_n=t_n-t_{n-1}$ the current step size, and $q_n$ the
order of a variable-step BDF formula. The multistep approximation satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=0}^{q_n}\alpha_{n,i}\mathbf y_{n-i}
  = h_n\dot{\mathbf y}_n,
  \label{eq:bdf_formula}
\end{equation}
where the coefficients $\alpha_{n,i}$ are determined by the order and the
history of the step sizes. Substitution into the residual DAE produces the
nonlinear algebraic problem
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf G_n(\mathbf y_n)
  \equiv
  \mathbf F\!\left(t_n,\mathbf y_n,
  \frac{1}{h_n}\sum_{i=0}^{q_n}\alpha_{n,i}\mathbf y_{n-i}\right)
  =\mathbf 0 .
  \label{eq:bdf_residual}
\end{equation}
Newton linearization of this problem gives the following result.
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J_n
  =\frac{\partial\mathbf G_n}{\partial\mathbf y_n}
  =\frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\mathbf y}
   +\gamma_n\frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\dot{\mathbf y}},
  \qquad
  \gamma_n=\frac{\alpha_{n,0}}{h_n}.
  \label{eq:newton_linearization}
\end{equation}
This relation is the mathematical link between the BDF time discretization and
the global Newton system. The numerical computations use the SUNDIALS IDA
solver as the backend for this residual DAE procedure \cite{hindmarsh2005sundials}.

\paragraph{Global unknown vector.}
The unknowns are grouped into a cell block, a trace block, and a capacitor
block,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf y
  =
  \bigl(\mathbf y_{\mathrm{cell}},\;
        \mathbf y_{\mathrm{trace}},\;
        \mathbf y_{\mathrm{pc}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:global_state}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf y_{\mathrm{pc}}
  =\bigl(P_{c,1},\dots,P_{c,N_{\mathrm{rcr}}}\bigr),
  \label{eq:capacitor_state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf y_{\mathrm{pc}}$ carries one capacitor pressure $P_{c,i}$ for
each \emph{capacitive} ($C_i>0$) RCR terminal. Purely resistive ($C_i=0$)
outlets impose pressure through algebraic conditions in $\mathbf R_{\mathrm{trace}}$.

\paragraph{Three-block residual.}
Stacking the cell weak form~\eqref{eq:hdg_local}, the transmission conditions,
and the Windkessel residual~\eqref{eq:rcr_capacitor} gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf F(t,\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y})
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  M_K\,\dot{\mathbf y}_{\mathrm{cell}}-\mathbf R_{\mathrm{cell}}(t,\mathbf y)\\[6pt]
  \mathbf R_{\mathrm{trace}}(t,\mathbf y)\\[6pt]
  \mathbf R_{\mathrm{pc}}(t,\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y}_{\mathrm{pc}})
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\mathbf 0 .
  \label{eq:global_dae_residual}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf R_{\mathrm{cell}}$ is the cell residual associated with the local weak form~\eqref{eq:hdg_local} (volume
flux, face flux via the trace, and source), $\mathbf R_{\mathrm{trace}}$
collects the interior, inflow, single-resistance, RCR, and junction transmission
residuals, and $\mathbf R_{\mathrm{pc}}$ is the capacitor
residual~\eqref{eq:rcr_capacitor}, which contains the time derivative
$C_i\,\dot P_{c,i}$. Thus, the time derivative enters only through the cell mass
matrix $M_K$ and the capacitor block; the trace rows are purely algebraic.

\textit{Differential / algebraic split.}
The rows carrying a time derivative are differential, the rest algebraic:
\begin{equation}
  \text{differential:} \;
  \{\mathbf y_{\mathrm{cell}}\}\cup\{\mathbf y_{\mathrm{pc}}\},
  \label{eq:differential_components}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{algebraic:}  \;
  \{\mathbf y_{\mathrm{trace}}\}.
  \label{eq:algebraic_components}
\end{equation}
The cell block and the capacitor block carry the time derivatives, whereas the
trace block is algebraic. Under the non-singularity condition established in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, the trace variables can be locally eliminated, so
the residual system~\eqref{eq:global_dae_residual} is a semi-explicit
index-one DAE.

\textit{Newton system.}
Using the notations in \eqref{eq:newton_linearization}, the derivative block is 
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\dot{\mathbf y}}
  =\mathrm{blockdiag}\bigl(M_K,\,\mathbf 0,\,\mathbf C_{\mathrm{wk}}\bigr),
  \qquad
  \mathbf C_{\mathrm{wk}}=\mathrm{diag}(C_i).
  \label{eq:dae_derivative_block}
\end{equation}
The Newton matrix has the form 
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J_n
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
   \gamma_n M_K + A & B & 0\\[4pt]
  C & D & E\\[4pt]
  0 & G & \gamma_n \,\mathbf C_{\mathrm{wk}}+\mathbf R_2^{-1}
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ida_Jblocks}
\end{equation}
% This saddle-point system \eqref{eq:ida_Jblocks} in which the differential unknowns are coupled to the algebraic trace unknowns. The leading block $\mathcal{A}$ collects the two differential contributions and is block-diagonal i.e., the $\alpha$ scaled cell mass matrix $\alpha M_k +A$ and the capacitor block $\alpha\,\mathbf C_{\mathrm{wk}}+\mathbf R_2^{-1}$, with $\mathbf C_{\mathrm{wk}}=\mathrm{diag}(C_i)$ the diagonal matrix of terminal capacitance, so that the cell and capacitor unknowns are uncoupled form each other. 
in which $A,B,C,D$ are the cell--cell, cell--trace, trace--cell and
trace--trace blocks already assembled for the network. The capacitor coupling reuses the boundary-section derivatives directly:

% The off-diagonal blocks $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ couples the differential and algebraic groups. They contain the cell-trace block $B$ and trace-cell block $C$, they contain the capacitor coupling whose entries are from the boundary-section directly:
\begin{equation}
  E \;\text{contains}\; E_{\mathrm{rcr}}
  =\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathbf B}_h^{\mathrm{rcr}}}{\partial P_c}
  =\begin{bmatrix}-1\\[2pt]0\end{bmatrix} ,
  \label{eq:rcr_trace_derivative}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  G \;\text{contains}\;
  \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{pc},i}}{\partial(\widehat A_i,\widehat U_i)}
  =\bigl(-\widehat U_i,\;-\widehat A_i\bigr),
  \label{eq:rcr_capacitor_derivative}
\end{equation}
% so that $E$ (trace-$P_c$) goes to $\mathcal{C}$ and $G$ ($P_c$-trace) in $\mathcal{B}$. The capacitor diagonal entry per outlet, which forms the lower
% block of $\mathcal A$, is
and the capacitor diagonal entry per outlet is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{pc},i}}{\partial P_{c,i}}
  +\gamma_n\,\frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{pc},i}}{\partial\dot P_{c,i}}
  =\frac{1}{R_{2,i}}+\gamma_n\,C_i .
  \label{eq:capacitor_newton_diagonal}
\end{equation}
The two mass terms $\gamma_n M_K$ and $\gamma_n C_i$ and the remaining entries comes from the residual Jacobian assembly. Here note the capacitance $C_i$ is distinct from the trace--cell block $C$.

This block structure is also the coupling interface for an external dynamical model. If an elasticity discretization introduces unknowns z and residuals $R_{\mathrm{ext}}(t, z, \dot{z}; y)$, 
 the coupled problem is obtained by augmenting
$ y$  and $F$ with the elastic variables and the interface equations.
The resulting off-diagonal blocks are assembled in the same Newton matrix \eqref{eq:capacitor_newton_diagonal}, providing a monolithic formulation for future external-elasticity coupling.

All computations and scaling results reported in this section are

24 threads) and 124\,GiB of RAM, running TUXEDO OS.
\subsection{Convergence Studies}
As a first verification test, we consider a manufactured solution to assess
the spatial accuracy of the proposed HDG method. The manufactured test case
is inspired by Example~5.1 of \cite{lucca2023semi}. We consider the
one-dimensional blood-flow equations~\eqref{equation_system} on a single
vessel of length $L=1\,\mathrm{m}$ and final time $T=0.1\,\mathrm{s}$. The
exact manufactured solution is chosen as
\begin{align*}
    A(x,t) &= A_d + \alpha_A\,(1 - \cos(2 \pi t))\, \sin(2 \pi x/L), \\
    U(x,t) &= \alpha_U\, \sin(2 \pi t)\, \cos(2 \pi x/L),
\end{align*}
where $\alpha_A = 5\times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m}^2$ and
$\alpha_U = 0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The corresponding source terms are obtained
by substituting these expressions into \eqref{equation_system}, so that the
manufactured solution satisfies the governing equations exactly. Unless
otherwise stated, all physical parameters are given in SI units. The
following parameters are used in the numerical experiments:

\emph{blood density} $\rho=1060$, \emph{viscosity} $\mu=0$,
\emph{wall thickness} $h_\mathrm{w}=1.2\times10^{-3}$,
\emph{Young's modulus} $E=4\times10^5$, \emph{reference pressure}
$P_0=0$, \emph{diastolic pressure} $P_d=9.46\times10^3$, and
\emph{diastolic cross-sectional area} $A_d=4.5239\times10^{-4}$. The errors
in the variables $A$ and $U$ are measured using the
$L^2$-norm and $H^1$-seminorm,
\begin{equation*}
\|e_w\|_{L^2}=\|w-w_h\|_{L^2},
\qquad
|e_w|_{H^1}=|w-w_h|_{H^1},
\qquad w\in\{A,U\}.
\end{equation*}

Table~\ref{tab:convergence}. The expected order of convergence is achieved
for both quantities $A$ and $U$ in the $L^2$ and $H^1$ norms.

\subsection{Verification with benchmark results}
\label{sec:verification}
In this section, we verify the proposed implicit HDG scheme against the
one-dimensional arterial blood-flow benchmark of Boileau et al.~\cite{boileau2015benchmark}. Of the test case proposed there, we consider two: a single aortic bifurcation and a $37$-artery network for which in~vitro pressure and flow
measurements are available. These two cases are chosen because together they cover both type boundary conditions used in this work: the aortic bifurcation is closed with full three-element (RCR) Windkessel models, whereas the $37$-artery network is terminated by single resistances. We reproduce both cases with the HDG method, using the polynomial
degrees and mesh refinements specified in Table~1 of
\cite{boileau2015benchmark}. In both cases, the governing $1\mathrm{D}$
equations are closed with the tube law~\eqref{tube_law}, using $A_d=A_0$ and
$P_d=0$, and the flow is started from
rest, $\big(A(x,0),U(x,0),P(x,0)\big)=\big(A_d(x),0,0\big)$. All geometric, wall and terminal parameters, together with the prescribed inflow $Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$, are taken directly from the supplementary data of \cite{boileau2015benchmark} .

\subsubsection{Aortic bifurcation}
\label{sec:aortic_bifurcation}
The first test case is a symmetric, single-bifurcation model of the abdominal
aorta dividing into the two iliac arteries~\cite{boileau2015benchmark}. Each
iliac outlet is coupled to a three-element ($RCR$) Windkessel model
representing the remainder of the systemic circulation. The outlet condition
is imposed through the terminal operator, while the aortic inlet is driven by
the \emph{in vivo} flow-rate signal $Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$ from the supplementary
data of the benchmark.

Figures~\ref{fig:aorta_results}--\ref{fig:iliac_results} compare the HDG waveforms of pressure, flow rate and luminal-radius change against the discontinuous Galerkin method (DCG) reference solutions at three locations: the midpoint of the aorta, the aortic bifurcation (junction point) and the midpoint of one iliac artery over the final periodic cycle, from $T = 28.6\,\mathrm{s}$ to $29.7\,\mathrm{s}$. At every location the HDG waveforms are visually indistinguishable from the
reference DCG results, reproducing both the systolic pressure peak and the reflected wave in diastole.

Following the benchmark configuration, we use polynomial degree $3$ with no
mesh refinement and employ $\mathrm{BDF}$ of maximum order $2$. Running to a
final time of $30\,\mathrm{s}$ with $6000$ time steps, our solver completes the
simulation in optimal state (release mode) $8.741\,\mathrm{s}$ debug mode $57.04\,\mathrm{s}$ on a single processor.

\subsubsection{Thirty-seven-artery network}
\label{sec:37artery}

The second test is the $37$-artery network of the largest central systemic
arteries, realised in~vitro as a set of silicone tubes and used as a
$1\mathrm{D}$
benchmark in~\cite{boileau2015benchmark}. At the inlet of the ascending aorta
the flow rate measured in~vitro is prescribed as $Q_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$, and every
terminal vessel is closed with a single-resistance outlet, i.e. the $C\to0$,
$R_1\to0$ limit of the Windkessel operator of Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}, with
$R_2$ the terminal resistance of the benchmark. We take the constant area to be
the average of the inlet and outlet areas, so that
$A_d=\pi(r_{\mathrm{in}}^2+r_{\mathrm{out}}^2)/2$ enters the tube law.

Following the benchmark configuration~\cite[Table~1]{boileau2015benchmark}, we
use polynomial degree $3$ and employ $\mathrm{BDF}$ of maximum order $3$.
Running to a final time of $20\,\mathrm{s}$ with $2000$ time steps, our solver
completes the simulation in $11.121\,\mathrm{s}$ in Release mode
$124.4\,\mathrm{s}$ in Debug mode on a on a single core of the workstation described above.

Figures~\ref{fig:37_aorta}--\ref{fig:37_gen3} compare the HDG pressure and flow
waveforms with the in~vitro measurements and with the FVM reference solutions at the aortic root and at representative vessels of the first, second and third generations of bifurcations. The HDG scheme captures the pressure amplification and the progressive steepening of the wavefront toward the periphery, and reproduces the timing and magnitude of the reflected waves; the agreement with the in~vitro data is comparable to that of the FVM scheme, confirming that the HDG discretization of the coupled network, including the junction and terminal conditions, attains benchmark-level
accuracy.

\paragraph{Time resolution.}
We integrate the semi-discrete system using the implicit, variable-step, variable-order $\mathrm{BDF}$ methods described above. The time-resolution experiments use the same backend and residual formulation. To study the effect of the maximum $\mathrm{BDF}$ order on the computational cost, we fix the spatial polynomial degree
to $p=3$ and consider two levels of global mesh refinement. The
simulations are performed on a single processor with fixed absolute and relative error tolerances. The resulting wall-clock times and time-integration statistics are reported in Figure~\ref{fig:time_resolution} and Table~\ref{tab:bdf_statistics}.

For both mesh resolutions, the smallest computational cost is obtained when the maximum $\mathrm{BDF}$ order is set to two. This behaviour is closely related to the number of accepted time steps: restricting the method to $\mathrm{BDF}1$ requires substantially more time steps, while allowing $\mathrm{BDF}2$
significantly reduces the number of steps needed to reach the final time. Increasing the maximum order beyond two does not result in a further reduction in computational cost; see Figure~\ref{fig:time_resolution}. In particular, for the higher mesh refinement, the number of accepted steps increases noticeably for the higher maximum orders, accompanied by an increase in the total number of nonlinear iterations; see Table~\ref{tab:bdf_statistics}.

The results also demonstrate that the maximum $\mathrm{BDF}$ order is only an upper bound for the adaptive time integrator. The selected order may vary during the simulation depending on the estimated local truncation error and the solution history. For example, when higher maximum orders are permitted, the dominant order need not coincide with the maximum allowed order, as indicated by the statistics in Table~\ref{tab:bdf_statistics}. Thus, the observed computational cost is determined by the combined adaptive selection of the time step and $\mathrm{BDF}$ order.

The stability properties of the $\mathrm{BDF}$ family provide useful context for
this behaviour. $\mathrm{BDF}$1 and $\mathrm{BDF}$2 are $A$-stable, whereas the higher-order
$\mathrm{BDF}$ methods are $A(\alpha)$-stable \cite{ascher1998computer}. The observed cost reflects the combined adaptive selection of step size and order. The experiments show that, for this problem and the chosen
error tolerances, allowing a maximum $\mathrm{BDF}$ order of two gives the lowest
observed computational cost.

\subsubsection{Synthetic large-scale networks: 30k, 50k and 100k vessels}
\label{sec:large_scale}

In this section, we go beyond the liver network described in
Section~\ref{sec:liver}. We generate three synthetic vascular trees of
$30{,}001$, $50{,}000$ and $100{,}000$ vessels using \textsc{svVascularize}
\cite{updegrove2017simvascular}, which constructs space-filling binary trees by CCO.
For each of the $N$ segments, the generator returns the segment endpoints, the length $L_i$, the reference
(diastolic) radius $r_{d,i}$ and the terminal (design) volumetric flow $Q_i$;
the simulations use the generated tree morphology, connectivity, lengths, radii and design flows directly. All three networks are run to a
final time $T = 1\,\mathrm{s}$ with the direct solver \textsc{mumps}. For the
$30{,}001$-vessel tree we use polynomial degree $p=2$ with $4$ cells per vessel,
and for the two larger trees $p=3$ with $1$ cell per vessel. In each
case MPI process counts of $4$, $6$, $8$, $10$ and $12$ are tested.

\paragraph{Vessel wall and constitutive parameters.}
The segments produced by \textsc{svVascularize} have constant cross-section, so that
$r_{\text{in}}=r_{\text{out}}=r_{d}$ and the reference cross-section is constant
along each vessel,
\begin{equation}
  A_{d,i} \;=\; A_{0,i} \;=\; \pi\,r_{d,i}^{2},
  \label{eq:ls_area}
\end{equation}
with $A_{d,i}$ the diastolic area entering the tube law of
Section~\ref{sec:liver}, recalled here as
\begin{equation}
  P(A,x)-P_{0} \;=\; P_{d} \;+\; \frac{\beta(x)}{A_{d}(x)}
      \Big(A^{m}-A_{d}(x)^{m}\Big),
  \qquad
  \beta(x)=\tfrac{4}{3}\sqrt{\pi}\,E\,h_{w}(x),
  \quad m=\tfrac12 .
  \label{eq:ls_tubelaw}
\end{equation}
The external and diastolic reference pressures are set to $P_{0}=P_{d}=0$. The wall stiffness that enters $\beta$ is prescribed through an empirical radius law of the same exponential family used for the anatomically
detailed ADAN networks \cite[Sec. 5.2.2]{MR5106706}, where the relative wall
thickness follows $h_{0}/R_{0}=a\,e^{b R_{0}}+c\,e^{d R_{0}}$. Here we express
the law directly in terms of the wall stiffness $E h_{w}/r_{d}$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{E\,h_{w}(r_{d})}{r_{d}} \;=\; k_{1}\,e^{k_{2}\,r_{d}} + k_{3},
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  h_{w}(r_{d}) \;=\; \frac{r_{d}}{E}\Big(k_{1}\,e^{k_{2}\,r_{d}}+k_{3}\Big),
  \label{eq:ls_wall}
\end{equation}
so that the elastic coefficient in \eqref{eq:ls_tubelaw} becomes
$\beta_i=\tfrac{4}{3}\sqrt{\pi}\,r_{d,i}\big(k_{1}e^{k_{2}r_{d,i}}+k_{3}\big)$
and depends on the local radius alone. A single reference modulus $E=2.25\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is assigned to every segment. The fit coefficients are
$k_{1}=6.5383\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa}$,
$k_{2}=-4.6154\times10^{2}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and
$k_{3}=2.6326\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{Pa}$; the resulting $\beta_i$ and $h_{w,i}$
are stored per vessel, together with $A_{d,i}$, $L_{i}$ and $r_{d,i}$.

\textit{Terminal boundary parameters.}
Every terminal vessel is closed with the Windkessel outlet of
Section~\ref{sec:liver}, so that at each outlet the trace satisfies
$P(\widehat A)=R_{1}\widehat A\,\widehat U+P_{c}$ together with the capacitor
equation $C\dot P_{c}=\widehat A\,\widehat U-(P_{c}-P_{\text{out}})/R_{2}$. The
total peripheral resistance of each terminal is set from the design flow
returned by the generator,
\begin{equation}
  R^{i}_{\text{tot}} \;=\; \frac{\Delta P}{Q_{i}},
  \label{eq:ls_Rtot}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta P=1.25\times10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ is the perfusion pressure
prescribed across the peripheral bed. The total resistance is split between the
proximal and distal resistors and the compliance is fixed through an $RC$ time
constant $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  R_{1}^{i}=\gamma\,R^{i}_{\text{tot}},\qquad
  R_{2}^{i}=(1-\gamma)\,R^{i}_{\text{tot}},\qquad
  C^{i}=\frac{\tau}{R^{i}_{\text{tot}}},
  \label{eq:ls_rcr}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=0.2$, $\tau=0.283\,\mathrm{s}$ and outlet pressure
$P_{\text{out}}=0$. The pure-resistance terminal used for the scaling runs
reported below is recovered as the $C\to0$, $R_{1}\to0$ limit of
\eqref{eq:ls_rcr}, i.e. $R_{1}^{i}=0$, $R_{2}^{i}=R^{i}_{\text{tot}}$ and
$C_{i}=0$, which is exactly the single-resistance special case of the outlet
operator in Section~\ref{sec:liver}. The single arterial inlet ($\mathrm{bid}=0$)
is driven by the prescribed inflow $Q_{\text{in}}(t)$; the remaining nodes are
tagged as terminals ($\mathrm{bid}=1,\dots,n_{\text{out}}$, one per outlet) or as
interior junctions, and only terminal nodes carry nonzero $(R_{1},R_{2},C)$. In
summary, all geometric and flow quantities
$\{L_i,\,r_{d,i},\,A_{d,i},\,Q_i\}$ are taken precisely from
\textsc{svVascularize}, while the material constants
$(E,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})$, the terminal constants $(\Delta P,\gamma,\tau)$ and the
reference pressures $(P_{0},P_{d},P_{\text{out}})$ are the only modelling inputs;
these are collected in Table~\ref{tab:ls_params}.

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented an HDG--HLL discretization for one-dimensional blood flow
on complex arterial networks with Riemann-coupled junctions and Windkessel
terminals. The hybrid traces place the network transmission conditions and the
terminal variables in a common global system, while the spatially discrete
problem has an explicit differential--algebraic structure: cell and capacitive
terminal variables are differential, whereas the network traces are
algebraic. This structure allows the complete flow network to be advanced through
one global residual and Jacobian structure at each time step. The analysis establishes local solvability and a
semi-discrete energy estimate under the stated positivity and subcriticality
assumptions. Manufactured-solution tests, the aortic bifurcation and the
$37$-artery benchmark verify the accuracy and physiological behavior of the
method, while the large-network experiments demonstrate its parallel
scalability.

The DAE formulation is also the principal interface for future multiphysics
extensions. In particular, an external nonlinear elasticity model can be
introduced by augmenting the flow residual with elastic unknowns and interface
equations, thereby retaining a single monolithic Newton system. This is
consistent with geometrical multiscale models in which one-dimensional
pressure and area data drive a three-dimensional elastic problem
\cite{heltai2021multiscale,belponer2023reduced}. Future work will
address the two-way exchange between the flow tube law and the external
elastic response, as well as the corresponding fully coupled analysis and
preconditioning strategy.
